Labeling the Microwave in Spanish

Jasmine's Journey from China to Tijuana

This article is part of the ESC Club's "Crossing Borders, Building Futures" oral history project, documenting the experiences of Chinese families living in and around the San Diego-Baja California border region. The ESC Club is a student-driven organization operating under the nonprofit Amazing Grace Institute, dedicated to multicultural communication and STEM education.

By the ESC Club | Crossing Borders, Building Futures Oral History Project

When Jasmine's family first arrived in Tijuana, she couldn't read a single label in her own kitchen. The microwave, the rice cooker, every appliance in the house — all marked in Spanish, a language she didn't yet speak. So she did what any resourceful teenager might: she cut up strips of tape, wrote the Spanish word for each button in her own hand, and stuck them next to every switch and setting in the house — not just for herself, but so her parents could understand them too.

It's a small, vivid image. But it says everything about how Jasmine built a life in a country she arrived in barely able to say "hola."

An Only Child, Headed Somewhere New

Born in the 1990s, Jasmine grew up as an only child in China. Her parents ran their own business, but for personal reasons, they made the decision to move abroad — and Jasmine, still a teenager, went with them to see what the outside world held.

Before she ever set foot in Mexico, her picture of the country came almost entirely from television: desert landscapes, cactus fields, a hot sun, people with deeply tanned skin. She assumed people spoke Spanish, and maybe a little English. In preparation, she attended a short summer-style Spanish language course back in China and picked up a few basic textbooks — including one of the only properly published, systematic Spanish-language books she could find in China at the time, since Spanish-learning materials were still scarce there in the early 2010s.

She packed dictionaries, a few phrasebooks of common spoken expressions, and not much else. Everything else, she figured, she'd pick up once she arrived.

Arrival: Golden Lights, Then a Different Reality

Jasmine still remembers her first sight of Tijuana — arriving at dusk, an entire hillside lit up with warm golden light bulbs, spread out like scattered stars. It was beautiful.

The next morning told a different story. The neighborhood she was taken to reminded her, she said, of China in the 1980s or '90s — low, flat-roofed houses, wooden walls, simple wooden doors, narrow streets that weren't fully paved. Her family settled in a neighborhood with a concentrated Chinese community, partly for comfort, but mostly for safety — not knowing the language yet, they were hesitant to live anywhere they couldn't lean on people who understood their situation.

Without any local connections, the family relied heavily on their landlord just to navigate daily life — how to buy groceries, how to pay for water, electricity, and internet.

The hardest adjustment, Jasmine said, wasn't any single event. It was the cumulative shift — leaving behind a comfortable life in China for a country that, at the time, felt genuinely short on resources.

"It's not like now, where global shipping makes everything reachable. Back then, it wasn't that developed yet."

Starting Over — Literally

Jasmine arrived in Mexico around age sixteen. What followed was nearly two years of uncertainty — a period she describes as simply "watching and waiting," unsure whether she'd stay in Mexico or eventually return to China. Originally, the plan hadn't even been Mexico at all: her family had relatives in Los Angeles, and Mexico was meant to be a temporary stop while the family waited to see if immigration paperwork to the US would come through. But their financial situation shifted after they arrived, and returning to China or continuing on to the US both became impractical. Mexico became home by circumstance as much as by choice.

After that period of limbo, Jasmine made a decision: she picked her studies back up, enrolling in a private school and restarting high school from the beginning, sitting in classrooms with local Mexican students her age. Because she wasn't a Mexican citizen, enrolling required translating and certifying all of her academic records into Spanish, and proving her prior education level before the school would admit her.

From that first day of high school through university graduation, the process took seven years total — three years of high school, four years of college. Counting from her arrival to today, in 2026, she said, it's been about seventeen years in total.

Learning With a Dictionary in Hand

Walking into a formal classroom as the only Chinese student — and one of very few Asian students at the school at all — was, by her own account, one of the hardest parts of the whole experience.

"At first, I could really only follow maybe twenty percent of what people were saying."

The school allowed her to bring a dictionary to class, and for a long time, that's how she got through each day: translating word by word, copying things down, scribbling small notes in the margins to keep track of meanings.

Some classmates were curious about her — she was, after all, one of the first Asian girls many of them had ever gone to school with. Some kept their distance. Others were warm and went out of their way to help, gesturing things out, explaining meanings with their hands when words weren't enough.

Academically, she found the material itself wasn't the challenge — much of what her new classmates were learning, she'd already covered back in China. The real task was building relationships and finding her footing socially. She started small: connecting with a handful of classmates, trading help back and forth — she'd help someone with math, they'd help her with Spanish. Slowly, by listening to how people spoke, watching how they expressed themselves, and picking up the more animated, expressive way locals communicated, her Spanish began to take shape.

Becoming the Family's Translator — At Sixteen

Jasmine's independence wasn't really optional. Her parents, older and less comfortable navigating an unfamiliar culture, leaned on her early and often. Within about a year of starting school, she was already setting up utility accounts, registering phone service, and — notably — teaching her own parents which bus routes to take.

"I had to push myself forward a step, to help them get things done, to help them adjust better to life here. I was young — I could take on the challenge in a way they couldn't as easily."

She's candid that this independence was, in her words, partly forced by circumstance rather than simply chosen. As an only child, there was no sibling to share the responsibility with. The support network she'd had in China — friends, extended family, a whole web of relationships — was largely severed the moment she left.

Growing Up Bilingual, Thinking in Two Languages

Jasmine described a particular kind of mental effort that came with operating in her second language: composing a thought fully in Mandarin first, then translating it into Spanish before speaking it aloud. Over time, that translation step began to disappear. Chinese sentence structure and Spanish sentence structure slowly merged into something more fluid, until she could speak Spanish without mentally drafting it in Chinese first.

Interestingly, she noted that this fusion cut both ways — after years of thinking bilingually, she said, her Mandarin sentence structure occasionally gets a little scrambled too. Spanish has effectively become her second native language.

Financially self-sufficient work started early as well. As a minor, she depended on her parents for spending money. But by eighteen, while in her first year of high school (repeated, per the Mexican system), she began working informally at a Chinese restaurant, prepping vegetables, earning her own income so she wouldn't add to her parents' financial burden. By her sophomore year of university, she landed a more formal position — a Chinese-owned lumber company needed a Mandarin-speaking employee, and she was referred in through community connections. For a while she worked mornings and attended classes in the afternoon; after graduating, the job became full-time, and her financial footing gradually stabilized.

Family, Roots, and Where Home Is Now

Today, Jasmine's immediate family — her parents, her husband, and her young son — all live in Mexico. Extended family, including aunts, uncles, and more distant relatives, remain in China; none of them have emigrated. Most of Jasmine's close family in Mexico now holds long-term residency or Mexican citizenship, and many run their own restaurants or businesses, with stable, settled lives.

She doesn't plan to move back to China to live — at most, she imagines visiting for travel, to reconnect with places and people from her early life.

Toward the end of the conversation, Jasmine turned a few questions back on her interviewers, curious whether they were second- or third-generation immigrants themselves, and how they'd navigated growing up bicultural in the US. She's already thinking ahead for her four-year-old son — asking about bilingual school systems, curious how to set him up for a future that might eventually include the United States. Her own mother, she mentioned, is a trilingual elementary school teacher and offered to share her contact information for anyone with similar questions.

A Life Built in Translation

Jasmine's story is, in many ways, a story of constant translation — not just of language, but of an entire life. Documents translated for school enrollment. Appliance labels translated with tape and a marker. Her own thoughts translated from Mandarin into Spanish, sentence by sentence, until translation itself stopped being necessary.

What stands out most is how young she was when most of that responsibility landed on her shoulders — sixteen, seventeen, eighteen years old, and already the one steering her family through an unfamiliar country. She doesn't dramatize it. She talks about it, matter-of-factly, as simply what needed to be done.

"I'm doing pretty well now."

It's a quiet answer, given near the close of the interview when asked how she was managing the pressures of building an entirely new life so young. But considering everything it took to get there, it carries real weight.
