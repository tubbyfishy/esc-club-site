Starting Over in the Fifth Grade

Shelley Zhu's Journey from Guangdong to Tijuana

This article is part of the ESC Club's "Crossing Borders, Building Futures" oral history project, documenting the experiences of Chinese families living in and around the San Diego-Baja California border region. The ESC Club is a student-driven organization operating under the nonprofit Amazing Grace Institute, dedicated to multicultural communication and STEM education.

By the ESC Club | Crossing Borders, Building Futures Oral History Project

Shelley Zhu was fourteen, about to turn fifteen, when she landed in Mexico for the first time. It was nighttime, and the city lights took her breath away. The next morning, she woke up to a different feeling entirely.

"I just got shocked. Where I arrived, there was a total difference from where I lived. The people, the language — it's typical for the first time."

That gap between the wonder of arrival and the shock of the morning after captures something true about Shelley's whole story: a life built by pushing forward through discomfort, one unfamiliar day at a time, until the unfamiliar became home.

A Grandfather's Search for a Better Future

Shelley's family came to Mexico from Guangdong province, in southern China, not far from Hong Kong. Like so many Chinese families who eventually put down roots in Tijuana, the journey started with one relative going first.

"My grandfather came to Mexico first for a good future," Shelley explained. "We didn't have enough money for living in China. I think this was the main reason — maybe for the economic situation we lived in."

By 2001, the rest of the family had followed, and Shelley — a teenager who had already finished seventh grade in China — arrived in a country where she couldn't say "hola" or "gracias."

Held Back to Move Forward

One of the more striking details of Shelley's story is what happened to her education when she arrived. Despite having completed seventh grade in China, the language barrier meant starting over — all the way back in fifth grade.

"I finished seventh grade in China, but due to the language, I didn't know Spanish. So I go back to the fifth grade. I repeated years, something like that."

She was, by her own account, the oldest student in every class she sat in from that point through high school. It was, in her words, simply "weird." But it worked. Immersed daily among much younger classmates speaking only Spanish, she absorbed the language in a way a classroom alone couldn't have taught her.

"It helped me a lot to learn how to connect with Latin culture and Spanish," she said. It took about three years before she felt she could speak Spanish comfortably and confidently.

Working Before She Could Legally Work

Shelley started working part-time as early as sixth or seventh grade, in a Chinese restaurant, starting out as a cashier — a job she's candid about not particularly enjoying. She kept working through middle school, high school, and into her college years, eventually moving into part-time work as an assistant at a small accounting firm.

"I felt exhausted," she said, describing those years. "There was no time for entertainment — just school, work, school, work. I don't think people like this kind of life."

But she doesn't frame those years as wasted or purely difficult. They built something.

"Learning and working — it helps me complete my career," she said. "I got more experience. But for the economics, the Mexican salary is lower than in the US. I don't think it's a good job for getting money. But for getting experience, it's good."

Asked for a favorite memory from that period, she didn't hesitate: the day she got her very first paycheck.

"I just felt like, wow, I have a lot of money."

She laughed telling it. She doesn't remember what she spent it on. She remembers exactly how it felt.

Being Called "Chino" on the Street

Not every part of adjusting to Mexico was gentle. Shelley described regularly being called "chino, chino, chino" by strangers on the street — a moment she processes with striking honesty and nuance.

"It's not that it makes me angry," she said, "but it's not polite, to see people from other countries — but that's a different culture. For Mexican people, it's normal to call you Chinese. But when we're Chinese and we hear that, don't you feel a little sad?"

It's a rare, unguarded reflection — someone naming a small daily wound without turning it into resentment, and without pretending it didn't happen either.

At the same time, she's quick to point out that the sting of that experience never defined her time in Mexico as a whole.

"I found other kinds of people. I have friends, I know their families," she said. "Not all of Mexico is like that. You can find the circle of people you like to be friends with."

What She Came to Love About Mexico

If there's one thing Shelley speaks about with unmistakable warmth, it's the sense of community she found among Mexican families and friends.

"Mexican culture has more of a concept like family. They like parties, they like to drink, but they get closer — with their bodies, with family, with friends. The one thing I really like about them is they like to help other people."

Her favorite Mexican dish is pozole. She also loves tacos, quesadillas, and tostadas — though, when asked whether she prefers Mexican or Chinese food, her answer was simple: both. (Korean barbecue, it turns out, is a close third.)

From Cashier to Controller

After high school, Shelley went on to study accounting in college — a decision she says clearly benefited her career. From there, her path moved through a small accounting firm, into earning her CPA license, and eventually into industry, where she now works as a controller overseeing the accounting departments of two maquiladora businesses in Tijuana.

She's watched the field itself change around her. Years ago, she explained, accounting jobs in Tijuana's industrial sector didn't require a college degree. That's no longer true — and the regulatory landscape keeps shifting too.

"The laws in Mexico, the fiscal rules — it's changing every day," she said. "I need to keep updating just to get the job, or to keep the job."

Asked whether her Chinese background ever played a role in her work, she pointed to something specific: understanding manufacturing procedures on the production side was harder for her than the accounting itself, which came naturally.

Now, as a controller, her days involve reviewing weekly cash flow, checking her coworkers' work, managing monthly tax filings, and preparing documentation for CPA reviews. What once felt overwhelming, she says, has become manageable — she's learned how to divide her time.

A Shrinking Community

Shelley has watched Tijuana's Chinese community change shape over the years. She had Chinese classmates in middle school. By high school, she says, there were none. In college, maybe one or two, out of her entire academic career.

"In Tijuana there used to be a lot of Chinese. But over the years, some moved back to China, some moved to other parts of Mexico. Now it's not as big as before. The community is still here, but it's shrunk."

It's a quiet observation, but a meaningful one — a reminder that immigrant communities aren't static. They grow, scatter, and reshape themselves across generations and geography.

Family, China, and What "Missing Home" Means

Asked whether she missed China after moving, Shelley's answer resists a simple yes or no.

"Yes and no," she said. "No, because all my family is here in Mexico. But I miss the places where I grew up — that part of memories. My family is here now, so I would go back to China just for vacation."

Her mother remains in China, and Shelley admits she hasn't been back to visit since she left. It's something she says she wishes she could do, if time and logistics ever allowed it.

Among her future travel hopes, China tops the list — followed by Japan and a trip to Europe.

Advice to Her Younger Self

When asked what she'd change about her journey, Shelley considered, for a moment, an entirely different path.

"Maybe if I took another career, like medicine, that would be totally different," she said. But medicine, she noted, was expensive and would have taken far longer — a road she didn't ultimately choose.

Rather than dwelling on the alternate version of her life, her advice to her younger self was simple and grounded:

"Just keep doing what you want to do."

Closing in Spanish

Near the end of the interview, when thanked for her time, Shelley responded not in English, but in Spanish — a small, generous gesture that said as much about her as any of her answers did.

"No, gracias a ustedes por invitarme y darme esa oportunidad para escucharme," she said. "Porque, bueno, soy una persona en el mundo, así que es mi honor tener la invitación de ustedes para compartir mi historia con todos."

("No, thank you for inviting me and giving me this opportunity to be heard. Because, well, I'm a person in this world, so it's my honor to have your invitation to share my story with everyone.")

Shelley Zhu's story is one of repeated resets — repeating a grade, restarting in a new language, rebuilding a career from cashier to controller. But each restart moved her forward, not backward. And more than a decade after arriving in Tijuana barely able to say hello, she's not just fluent in Spanish — she's fluent in a life she built entirely on her own terms.
